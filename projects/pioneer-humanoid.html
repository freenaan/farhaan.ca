Overview

Humanoid robotics is usually a graduate-level research topic. It takes fields like ML, simulation, and mechanical design, turns up the difficulty, and squeezes them all together. But they’re just too cool not to build, so I decided to try and make one anyway.

That’s why I joined WATonomous Humanoid, to help prove that a few questionably trained undergrads can make a life-sized humanoid capable of walking and interacting with the world all on its own. During my co-op I designed and manufactured two revisions of our bimanual arm, designed an automatic synthetic data generation pipeline for imitation learning, and architected the power system to drive our (very power hungry) actuators.

6DOF Arm(s)

Even the most robust RL policies mean nothing without hardware to deploy it on. Our simulation team was going strong by the time I joined, but our hardware was extremely limited: two fingers and a few fragile shoulder parts. To fix this, I finalized ArmV1 along with a rudimentary wiring harness. Despite the scrappy appearance, this arm was deceptively strong. We had multiple uncontrolled inverse-kinematics deployments on it, one of which ripped the threads right out of an actuator before the 3D-printed PLA shoulder gave way. It still had real issues though, including bending moments at the actuator and its overall visual appeal.

Enter ArmV2, a ground-up redesign that adds straddle support (a bearing opposite the rotor) and integrated spider couplings (for angular deflection) to the major joints, preventing bending moments from damaging screw threads or internal bearings; dedicated anchor points that eliminate wire snag and enable high-ROM manipulation; and, frankly, just looks better than before.

It still isn’t perfect. Next term’s plans include fully internal cable routing, a subframe, and a proper surface finish, but for now it’s a genuinely usable bimanual arm system that can run manipulation tasks.

CuRobo Synthetic Datagen + ACT Policy

Manual demos are a pain, so why not automate it? I paired Nvidia’s CuRobo collision-free motion planner with privileged simulation ground-truth states to procedurally generate the arm’s trajectory, all dockerized on Linux. To ensure robustness, I added heavy domain randomization (arm sag, offset position recovery, jittery actuators and sensors) for recovery experience outside nominal trajectories.

Using this, I trained a visuomotor policy that completes the pick-and-place task in sim. An ACT policy was chosen because action chunking adds a look-ahead horizon that predicts the next steps and reduces jitter. For future iterations I could move from discrete waypoints to a more continuous trajectory, since policies generally handle smoother gradients and motion better.

VR Teleop

Simulation data handles the basics, but generalizing to messy real-world tasks needs human intuition. Our baseline VR teleop stack ran at an unusable 0.14 real-time factor (RTF), so I took over the pipeline to make it fluid enough for real data collection. It streams WebXR spatial packets from a Meta Quest 2 over JSON to a C++ node, which translates them into ROS2 positions subscribed to by Nvidia Isaac Sim, all containerized with Docker on Linux.

To crush the latency bottleneck, I re-architected the infrastructure. Container consolidation collapsed two separate Docker containers into one, killing inter-container network overhead, and direct tensor access bypassed the slow image-broadcasting step by reading Isaac Sim’s raw image tensors straight into the VR view. Together, these changes slashed compute time per frame by ~3x, taking us from a lagging 0.14 RTF to a stable, real-time 1.0 RTF with headroom to spare, comfortably fast enough to capture fluid human data for training.
